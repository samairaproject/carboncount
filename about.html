About CarbonCount

An everyday tracker that treats accessibility as part of the climate answer

CarbonCount is a lightweight travel-logging app built on a simple idea: the "greener" choice only counts if you can actually take it. Most carbon-tracking tools assume everyone can walk to the train, bike across town, or take the stairs when an elevator is down. This app starts from a different assumption — that low-carbon transportation has to be accessible transportation, or it isn't really a solution at all.

About the app

What the app does

CarbonCount gives you three simple tools:

  • Daily Log — record each trip you take, describe it in your own words, enter the miles, and see an estimated carbon impact build up over the day.
  • Profile — save a short bio, note any accessibility or mobility needs you want the app to account for, and flag which lower-carbon travel modes actually work for you.
  • The Problem, below — background on why climate impact and accessibility are connected, not separate issues.

Right now, everything you enter is saved locally in your browser — nothing is uploaded to a server. That keeps the current version simple and private, though it also means your data won't follow you to a different device yet.

How the numbers work

Your Daily Log gives a carbon estimate per mile for each travel mode, based on published national averages: driving is estimated at about 0.89 lbs CO₂ per mile (EPA average for a typical passenger vehicle), public transit at about 0.45 lbs (FTA national average across bus and rail), accessible paratransit-style routing at about 0.10 lbs, and walking or biking at effectively zero tailpipe emissions. See the "How we calculate this" box on the Route Planner page for the full formula and sources. These are still simplified averages meant to show relative impact, not a precise personal carbon audit.

What's next

This is an early, working version of the app. Planned next steps include real accounts so your log and profile sync across devices, live accessibility data for transit routes, and weekly summaries that connect your logged trips to regional climate and transit policy — the kind of context you can read about now in The Problem, below.

The problem it solves

Why this app exists

The Climate Crisis & Accessible Infrastructure

Climate change is usually discussed in terms of global temperatures and emissions curves. But at the street level, it shows up as something much more concrete: which routes are open, which elevators are running, and which neighborhoods get rebuilt first after a storm.

Two problems, one map

Transportation is a major source of household carbon emissions, and shifting people toward buses, trains, walking, and biking is one of the most effective ways to cut it. But that shift only works if the lower-carbon option is actually usable. A subway line with a broken elevator isn't a green choice for a wheelchair user — it's simply unavailable, pushing that trip back onto a car. Every accessibility gap in low-carbon infrastructure quietly increases emissions by removing an option, not just an inconvenience.

Instability raises the stakes

When regional or national instability disrupts funding, maintenance, or planning, transit systems tend to defer the repairs that keep them accessible first — ramps, elevators, curb cuts, and paratransit services are often the first budget lines cut and the last ones restored. That means climate vulnerability doesn't fall evenly. Disabled residents can be left with fewer safe evacuation routes during extreme weather, longer waits for accessible transit during service disruptions, and less flexibility to adapt when a familiar route suddenly stops working.

Why CarbonCount treats them together

Most climate apps track emissions. Most accessibility tools track routes. Very few do both at once — which means the people most affected by infrastructure gaps are often invisible in the data used to justify new climate policy. By logging trips that include accessibility context, this app aims to make that overlap visible: not just "how much carbon did this city save," but "who was actually able to make the low-carbon choice, and who wasn't."

What you can do here

Use your Profile to note the accessibility needs that matter for your own routing, and use the Daily Log to track which trips you were able to make sustainably versus which ones defaulted to driving. Over time, patterns in that data are exactly the kind of evidence that can support arguments for better-maintained, more accessible low-carbon infrastructure.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my data private?

Yes. Everything you enter — your bio, accessibility notes, and logged trips — is stored only in your browser's local storage. Nothing is sent to a server, and nothing is shared with anyone.

Will my log carry over to a new device or browser?

Not yet. Since data is stored locally, clearing your browser data or switching devices will reset your log. Account-based syncing is on the roadmap.

How accurate are the CO₂ estimates?

They're simplified per-mile averages meant to show relative impact between travel modes, not a precise personal carbon audit. Think of them as directional, not exact.

Do I have to share accessibility information?

No. Every field on your Profile page is optional, including the "Prefer not to say" option in the accessibility checklist. Sharing it just helps the app's suggestions fit you better.

Can I get my data back out of the app?

Yes — the Daily Log page has an "Export as CSV" button that downloads all of your logged trips as a spreadsheet-ready file.
